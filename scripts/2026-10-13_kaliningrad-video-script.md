# Kaliningrad and the Danger of Nuclear War

**Explainer video script, revised 13 October 2026**

Narration runs to roughly 1,400 words, about 9½ to 10½ minutes at 130–150 words per minute. Bracketed lines are cues for the editor and are not read aloud.

## Script

[COLD OPEN]
[ON SCREEN: map of the Baltic Sea, Kaliningrad highlighted, NATO states shaded]

On the southern shore of the Baltic Sea, between Poland and Lithuania, sits a piece of Russia cut off from the rest of the country. It is called Kaliningrad. About a million people live there. It is the headquarters of Russia’s Baltic Fleet and a base for its nuclear-capable missiles. On every side, by land and by sea, it is surrounded by NATO.

At the end of September, Russia warned that it would defend this territory with nuclear weapons.

The warning was issued through Russian embassies in Belgium, Britain and Ireland on 28 and 29 September, and in a formal diplomatic note to NATO. If the alliance tried to blockade or isolate Kaliningrad, the note said, Russia would use “the entire arsenal of forces and capabilities at its disposal, including nuclear weapons.” It added that a war could begin with strikes on “decision-making centres” in NATO countries.

On 1 October, Vladimir Putin repeated the warning in person. An escalating cycle of retaliation, he said, leads to “a fatal point of no return.”

[ON SCREEN: archive footage, Kennedy’s television address, October 1962]

In October 1962, when the United States and the Soviet Union came to the brink over missiles in Cuba, President Kennedy went on national television and the world followed every hour of the crisis. This time, days after Moscow’s warning, the *New York Times* and CNN had still not reported it.

This video explains how we got here, why the danger keeps growing, and what can stop it.

[PART ONE: HOW WE GOT HERE]
[ON SCREEN: animated map of NATO expansion, 1999 to 2024]

The story begins at the end of the Cold War. In February 1990, during talks on German reunification, US Secretary of State James Baker told Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev that NATO would not move “one inch eastward.” Other Western leaders gave similar assurances.

At the end of 1991 the Soviet Union was dissolved. The Stalinist bureaucracy, a privileged caste that had ruled in the name of socialism, restored capitalism, and a new class of oligarchs seized what had been state property.

NATO moved east. Poland, Hungary and the Czech Republic joined in 1999. The three Baltic states, Romania, Bulgaria, Slovakia and Slovenia followed in 2004. Finland joined in 2023 and Sweden in 2024. The Baltic Sea became a NATO lake. Kaliningrad, recognised as Russian territory since the Potsdam Conference of 1945, became an island inside the alliance.

The war in Ukraine grew out of three decades of this expansion. The United States and the European powers arm Ukraine, provide its targeting intelligence and pay for its long-range drones. Ukraine is the battlefield in a war to weaken Russia, subordinate it and open up its vast resources to Western capital.

None of this makes the Putin regime progressive. It speaks for the oligarchy that emerged from the restoration of capitalism. Its invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 was a reactionary response to NATO’s encirclement, and Ukrainian and Russian workers have paid for it with their lives.

[PART TWO: THE FLASHPOINT]
[ON SCREEN: timeline, August to October 2026]

What would a blockade of Kaliningrad mean in practice?

Through the summer, NATO staged air and naval exercises around the territory. In mid-August, according to a Western military source quoted by Reuters, dozens of NATO aircraft flew an unannounced operation around Kaliningrad. It showed they could cut its radio and radar links and disable its air defences. In September, Poland’s foreign minister, Radosław Sikorski, observed that Russia had moved most of its troops out of Kaliningrad to fight in Ukraine. He called for NATO to exercise so aggressively that Putin could not be sure what the alliance intended.

Under international law, a blockade is an act of war. The United Nations definition of aggression, adopted in 1974, lists the blockade of a state’s ports or coasts by another state’s armed forces. Russia’s nuclear doctrine, revised in November 2024, permits a nuclear response to a conventional attack that critically threatens its territory. That provision was written with Kaliningrad in mind.

[PART THREE: EUROPE’S ANSWER]
[ON SCREEN: Rutte, Merz and Jetten at Münster, 1 October]

Europe’s governments answered the warning by escalating.

NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte told a conference in Brussels, “I don’t take this seriously.” German Chancellor Friedrich Merz pledged to support Ukraine “with all our strength.” On 4 October he travelled to Kyiv with German corporate executives, including arms manufacturers, and announced the joint development of weapons with a range of more than 1,000 kilometres. The European Union’s defence commissioner, Andrius Kubilius, said Putin needed to “feel that war is coming to Russia itself.”

On 12 October, the EU added 1,569 Russian companies and executives to its sanctions lists, its largest single move yet against Russia’s arms industry. On 13 October, NATO began its annual nuclear exercise, Steadfast Noon, with 17 countries rehearsing nuclear operations. And Lithuania, which borders Kaliningrad, is moving to remove the clause in its constitution that bans weapons of mass destruction from its soil.

The pattern repeats. Each Russian warning is dismissed as bluff. Each threshold crossed without a response becomes the argument for crossing the next.

David North, chairman of the *World Socialist Web Site*’s International Editorial Board, has compared this to the years before 1914. The European powers passed through one crisis after another, each ending short of general war. In 1909 a weakened Russia backed down before Germany. In July 1914, Berlin assumed Russia would back down again. It did not.

[PART FOUR: WHY]
[ON SCREEN: archive images, 1914 and 1939]

Why do governments behave like this when the stakes include the survival of humanity?

Marxists answered that question more than a century ago, during the First World War. Lenin showed that capitalism had entered its imperialist stage. Giant monopolies and banks, fused with their states, compete to divide and redivide the world’s markets, raw materials and territory. Leon Trotsky, who led the Russian Revolution of 1917 with Lenin, identified the contradiction underneath. Production had become international, while the world remained divided into rival nation-states, each defending the profits of its own capitalists. Writing in 1914, he called the war “a revolt of the forces of production against the political form of nation and state.”

That contradiction produced two world wars. It has not gone away.

Germany is rearming to become Europe’s leading military power. The European powers want Ukraine’s land, minerals, energy and markets, and a dominant position in Eastern Europe. Washington, facing soaring energy prices, is pursuing its own arrangement with Moscow. On 9 October, Donald Trump announced a deal with Putin to resume Russian diesel exports and demanded that Ukraine replace President Zelensky. European governments called it a betrayal and stepped up their own offensive. Rivalry among the imperialist powers sharpens the danger.

War serves a purpose at home as well. Governments across Europe are cutting jobs, wages and public services to pay for rearmament, and they face growing resistance. In France, hundreds of thousands of workers and students have struck and marched against austerity in recent weeks. An enemy abroad is used to demand national unity and to justify repression at home.

[PART FIVE: WHAT CAN STOP IT]
[ON SCREEN: photograph of Bogdan Syrotiuk; freebogdan.org]

No government involved in this conflict will end it. The European powers are committed to escalation. The Putin regime offers nationalism and nuclear threats. It cannot appeal to the workers of Europe and America against their own governments, because it represents an oligarchy that fears its own working class.

The force that can stop the war is the international working class. Workers in Russia, Ukraine, Europe and the United States have no interest in this war. They do the fighting and the dying, and they pay for rearmament through austerity.

Bogdan Syrotiuk shows what that fight involves. He is a 27-year-old Ukrainian socialist and leader of the Young Guard of Bolshevik-Leninists. He was arrested in April 2024. On 10 August 2026, a court in Pervomaisk sentenced him to 15 years in prison for “high treason.” His offence was writing articles calling on Ukrainian and Russian workers to unite against the war. His lawyers have appealed.

The campaign to free Bogdan defends the right to oppose the war and the principle on which any anti-war movement must stand, the unity of workers across national borders.

The first task is to break the silence. Millions sense that something is wrong but cannot believe their governments would gamble with nuclear war. They are gambling with it now.

## Editorial notes

### Corrections against the sources

* The “not one inch eastward” assurance dates from February 1990, before the Soviet Union was dissolved. The original placed it in 1991.
* The original said the warning was a single note delivered through embassies. The embassy statements (28–29 September) and the diplomatic note to NATO were separate; both are now named. Putin’s confirmation was at the Valdai Club on 1 October.
* The original described a “largest sanctions package yet” of “more than 1,500 companies and executives”. The source gives 1,569 listings (826 companies, 743 executives) and calls it the most extensive expansion against the arms industry, so the wording is narrowed.
* Steadfast Noon began on 13 October, in Italy, so it is now dated.
* Lithuania’s amendment has passed a first vote (99 to 13, 22 September) and needs further votes. “Is moving to remove” reflects that.
* Bogdan Syrotiuk was sentenced on 10 August 2026 by the Pervomaisk City District Court. The verdict is under appeal (filed 9 September) and has not entered into legal force, so the script says he was sentenced and has appealed.
* “The most dangerous place on Earth” was dropped as unsupported. The Cuba comparison does the same work and is sourced.

### Political and theoretical changes

* The original called the war “a conflict between imperialist powers”. The WSWS characterises it as a US-NATO war against Russia, with the Putin regime’s invasion a reactionary response. Equating the two camps is the position the WSWS has criticised in the pseudo-left. The script now follows the WSWS characterisation, and keeps the inter-imperialist analysis for the rivalry among the US and European powers, illustrated by the Trump–Putin diesel deal.
* “Both represent the same class enemy” was replaced with North’s argument that the Putin government cannot appeal to workers abroad because it represents the oligarchy.
* Lenin’s theory of imperialism is added alongside Trotsky, and the 1914 analogy from North’s article gives the theory a concrete historical illustration.
* The domestic function of war (austerity, repression, France) is added to connect the danger to viewers’ own conditions.

### Check before recording

* The Trotsky quotation is from the English translation of *The War and the International* (1914). I am confident of the wording but it should be checked against the Marxists Internet Archive text.
* The Baker quotation and NATO accession dates are from general knowledge, not the WSWS articles. They are well documented but worth a glance.
* The New York Times and CNN point refers to the position as of 30 September. If the video goes out much later, add “at first”.
* The French protest figures are deliberately general (“hundreds of thousands”). The WSWS gives almost half a million on 6 October if you want a number.
